About · The shows

How it's made

Every show is one Python file. It computes the lights frame by frame, writes the music note by note from the same timings, and carries the line that's spoken before it. Here's how that file becomes a show on the roofline, and on your phone.

From an idea to the roofline

Each sequence is one Python file with two functions that share the same timings. render() computes the lights and score() writes the music, so every hit lands on its flash and is panned to where it happens on the house. A VOICEOVER line gives it a spoken intro.

The house itself comes from two exceptional open-source projects: I laid out the pixels in xLights, and modeled the house in Blender. Pixelhome imports both, so every sequence knows where each pixel sits in 3D. Sequences find their way around the house by the names of its runs and distances along them, never by pixel numbers, so when a run is re-strung with more pixels, every show follows.

Music

A show I invent gets original music, written note by note in Python and played through great open-source instruments: the Surge XT hybrid synthesizer for pads, leads, bells and basses, and the VSCO 2 Community Edition orchestral samples. Spotify's pedalboard adds the reverb, compression and limiting. Because the score and the lights read the same timings, every hit lands on its flash, and each sound is panned to where it happens on the house.

A show that tells a real work plays that work's real music. Wagner's Ring operas come from the Wagner Ring Dataset, his score timed bar by bar to a recording: a script cuts the most dramatic bars of each scene, and Pixelhome orchestrates them for the samples. Then, the way a staged production would, every event the lights show gets a sound: thunder with the lightning, hoofbeats with the Valkyries, fire, clashing swords.

The oratorios (Mendelssohn's Elijah, Handel's Judas Maccabaeus and Messiah) have no dataset, so their notes are transcribed by hand from public-domain vocal scores. Scripts read the noteheads and staff lines off high-resolution scans, every bar is checked against its time signature, and the cut is timed by the score's own tempo markings. There's no choir in the sample library, so sung lines are played on orchestral instruments, each character with an instrument and a colour of their own.

Everything plays outdoors to the street, so melodies stay in the middle register, where they carry across a front yard. The all-night loops are kept soft, with no beat and nothing startling.

Spoken intros

One string of pixels is abstract. A shape moving along the roofline could be a bat, a train or a skeleton, and from the street you can't always tell which. So, the way an opera is introduced with a synopsis, a show can open with a short spoken intro: a line to set the mood, then what happens and where to look.

The voice is based on mine, modified with a bit of an accent. I recorded a few seconds of myself reading, and Chatterbox, Resemble AI's open-source text-to-speech model, reads each intro in that voice on my Mac. It's mixed over a quiet bed made for each occasion. While it speaks, the house lights up in the occasion's colour: the show's title runs as a Morse marquee along the left wing, each spoken word flashes as 8-bit ASCII on the porch front, and the voice grows as a waveform out from the middle of the right wing.

On this site, the Intros switch next to Sound plays a sequence's intro before the show. For FPP, each intro is its own FSEQ and MP3, scheduled just before the show it introduces.

From the repo to the house

Everything lives in one repository. A push builds the site in GitHub Actions: it re-renders only the sequences whose files changed, makes each one's 3D preview, share card and page, and works out the show times from the house's schedule. Nothing goes live until a release: a version tag on the main branch deploys the site.

The same schedule drives the house. A sync script writes it into FPP, a playlist for each kind of night and the whole season's calendar, uploads the sequences and music that changed, and removes anything the site no longer has. The house is never fully dark: soft loops keep it lit late at night, and by day a few bright pixels sparkle against the sun.

Live

Stand in front of the house, open pixelhome.app/live, and your phone shows what the lights are doing right now and plays the soundtrack in time with them. No video or audio is streamed. The site already has every sequence and every MP3, so all that has to travel is a few bytes: which file FPP is playing and how far into it.

That message goes through Cloudflare Workers. A small service on the controller reads FPP's status once a second and posts to the Worker when something changes (a new song, a start, a stop, a jump) and every 30 seconds as a heartbeat. The Worker hands it to a Durable Object, one per house, that keeps the latest state and pushes it over a WebSocket to every phone watching. Connections only go out from the house, so nothing at my house is open to the internet, and phones only listen. It all fits in Cloudflare's free plan: a night is about 2,000 small requests against a limit of 100,000 a day.

Getting past the latency

The hard part isn't knowing what's playing, it's knowing where it is to within a fraction of a second. Clocks on the controller, a server and a phone never quite agree, so nothing compares timestamps between them. Instead each delay is measured on its own:

  • House to Cloudflare. The controller keeps its connection open so a report never waits on a new TLS handshake, times each round trip, and adds half the quickest recent one to the position before sending.
  • Waiting at Cloudflare. A phone that joins mid-song gets the last report plus its age, so it adds however long the report sat there.
  • On the phone. From then on the phone runs its own clock and only needs a heartbeat to check it.

Sound is the trickier part. A phone takes a moment to start playing after it jumps to a new spot, and every jump is a gap you can hear. So the page aims a little ahead of the house, measures how late the music actually came out, and remembers that lead on your phone so the next song lands right the first time. It doesn't correct with a second jump; it only re-syncs if the music slips by more than a second. Once the music is playing, the lights on screen follow your phone's audio, not the network, so what you see always matches what you hear. Anything left over is about the same as the delay of sound crossing a front yard.
